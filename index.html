Flocking Behavior

Episode 01
Emergence Behavioral Ecology Multi-Agent

Based on Craig Reynolds' 1986 Boids model. Three simple rules generate complex collective movement — separation, alignment, cohesion.

Flocking Behavior — Craig Reynolds, 1986

In 1986, computer scientist Craig Reynolds published a landmark paper introducing "Boids" (Bird-oid Objects), a simulation model that reproduces the emergent collective movement observed in bird flocks, fish schools, and insect swarms.

Reynolds demonstrated that complex, lifelike group behavior arises from just three simple local rules applied to each individual agent:

  • Separation — avoid crowding neighbors (short-range repulsion)
  • Alignment — steer towards the average heading of nearby agents
  • Cohesion — move toward the average position of nearby agents

No central coordination. No global information. Pure emergence.

The predator-prey extension adds a fourth dynamic: escape behavior, where prey agents detect and flee from a predator, while the predator hunts using proximity-based targeting. This models biological predation patterns studied in behavioral ecology.

Reynolds, C. W. (1987). Flocks, herds and schools: A distributed behavioral model. ACM SIGGRAPH Computer Graphics, 21(4), 25–34.

Deterministic Chaos

Episode 02
Chaos Theory Classical Mechanics Sensitivity to Initial Conditions

A double pendulum — two arms, two masses, four numbers, zero randomness. The equations are written in Newton's hand. The future is unknowable.

Deterministic Chaos — Lorenz, 1961

In 1961, MIT meteorologist Edward Lorenz typed weather data into his computer rounded to three decimals instead of six. Three months of simulated weather later, his result was unrecognizable. He had stumbled onto something Newton, Laplace, and Einstein had all missed: a perfectly deterministic system that is, in principle, unpredictable.

The double pendulum is the cleanest demonstration. Two rigid arms hinged together, swinging under gravity, governed by the Lagrangian equations of motion. Given identical initial conditions, two identical pendulums will trace identical paths — forever. But change the initial angle by one ten-thousandth of a radian, and within seconds the two trajectories have nothing in common.

  • Determinism — same equations, same start → same future, exactly
  • Sensitivity — an angle off by 10⁻⁴ radians becomes a different trajectory in seconds
  • Unpredictability — long-term forecasting fails, not from noise, but from this sensitivity alone

This phenomenon — the foundation of modern chaos theory — appears wherever local rules generate global dynamics: weather, financial markets, neural firing, planetary orbits over millions of years. The same shape governs them all.

Lorenz, E. N. (1963). Deterministic Nonperiodic Flow. Journal of the Atmospheric Sciences, 20(2), 130–141. — Baker, G. L., & Gollub, J. P. (1990). Chaotic Dynamics: An Introduction. Cambridge University Press.

Living Patterns

Episode 03
Reaction-Diffusion Morphogenesis Emergence

A leopard's spots, a zebra's stripes, the bands on a tropical fish — Alan Turing reduced them all to two chemicals competing on a grid. Watch the pattern decide itself.

The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis — Turing, 1952

Two years before his death, Alan Turing — the man who broke Enigma and invented the universal computer — published a paper on biology. He asked a question nobody else was asking: where do the spots on a leopard come from? Why do zebras have stripes? Why are tropical fish painted with such precise patterns? The standard answer at the time was genetic — somewhere in the DNA, a blueprint specifies "spot here, stripe there." Turing suggested something stranger.

He proposed two chemicals — an "activator" A and an "inhibitor" B — diffusing across the developing embryo and reacting with each other. The activator produces more of itself AND of the inhibitor. The inhibitor degrades both. If the inhibitor diffuses faster than the activator, the uniform initial state becomes unstable, and stable spatial patterns spontaneously appear. No blueprint. Just two equations and a difference in diffusion rates.

Turing wrote them down in the Gray-Scott form below — two coupled reaction-diffusion equations. Slide the feed rate f and the kill rate k and you cross every animal in the savanna without changing a single line of code.

  • Activator–inhibitor system — two chemicals, one promotes itself, one suppresses both
  • Turing instability — uniform state becomes unstable when D_B > D_A
  • Universal pattern atlas — spots, stripes, mazes, mitosis, all on one (f, k) map
  • Predicted before observed — chemicals matching Turing's model were only identified in 2012

The same equations describe the patterns on the lionfish, the regular spacing of hair follicles in your skin, the ridges in a fingerprint, and the bands of melanin that draw a fingernail's structure. A single chemical instability, written down in 1952 by a man dying of injustice, that turned out to draw most of life's surface.

Turing, A. M. (1952). The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis. Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, 237(641), 37–72.

The Butterfly Equation

Episode 04
Strange Attractor Atmospheric Physics Butterfly Effect

Three coupled equations. Three variables. A trajectory that never repeats, never escapes — and is impossible to predict. The shape of weather.

The Lorenz Attractor — 1963

Edward Lorenz built a toy model of atmospheric convection — a heated fluid layer in a gravitational field. He kept only what he believed essential and reduced the Navier-Stokes equations to three coupled ordinary differential equations: dx/dt = σ(y−x), dy/dt = x(ρ−z)−y, dz/dt = xy−βz. Three variables. Two nonlinearities. No randomness.

What he discovered, plotted on his lab's printer, was a shape no one had seen before: a trajectory that orbits forever inside a bounded region of space without ever crossing itself, without ever settling, without ever repeating. A strange attractor.

  • Deterministic — same equations, same initial point → identical trajectories, forever
  • Bounded — the orbit never escapes a finite region of state space
  • Non-periodic — it never closes a loop, never repeats a previous point
  • Sensitive — two starts ε = 10⁻⁵ apart diverge by macroscopic amounts in seconds

This is the mathematical reason weather forecasting stops being useful past 10–14 days, no matter how powerful your computer. It is also the geometric signature of every system where local rules generate global dynamics that cannot be solved analytically — only watched.

Lorenz, E. N. (1963). Deterministic Nonperiodic Flow. Journal of the Atmospheric Sciences, 20(2), 130–141.

Galaxies from Gravity

Episode 05
N-Body Problem Gravitation Galaxy Formation

Hundreds of stars, one law — Newton's. No blueprint, no choreography. Let them pull on each other and a spiral galaxy assembles itself out of nothing.

The N-Body Problem — Newton, 1687

Newton gave us one line for gravity: every pair of masses attracts along the line between them, with a force F = G·m₁·m₂/r². For two bodies it solves cleanly — Kepler's ellipses. Add a third and the system becomes non-integrable: there is no general closed-form solution. For hundreds of bodies the only way forward is to compute every pairwise pull and step time forward, over and over.

That is exactly what this simulation does — a direct O(N²) sum each frame, integrated with a symplectic velocity-Verlet scheme so orbital energy stays honest. Nothing about a spiral is written into the rules. It emerges.

  • Emergent — spiral arms are density waves the disk grows on its own from tiny irregularities
  • Scale-free — the same law that drops an apple shapes a galaxy a hundred thousand light-years across
  • Chaotic — like the three-body problem, long-term trajectories are unpredictable in detail
  • Softened — a length ε smooths the 1/r² singularity so near-misses don't fling stars to infinity

Throw two of these disks at each other and they tear long tidal tails, then merge into one — the same process that built many of the galaxies we see today, including the collision the Milky Way and Andromeda are sliding into.

Newton, I. (1687). Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica. London: Royal Society.

When Looking Changes Reality

Episode 06
Quantum Mechanics Wave–Particle Duality Interference

Fire particles one at a time through two slits and they build up a wave interference pattern — as if each went through both. Try to catch which slit, and the pattern vanishes.

Young's Double Slit — 1801, and the heart of quantum mechanics

Thomas Young shone light through two narrow slits and saw stripes — bright and dark bands that only make sense if light is a wave, with crests and troughs that add and cancel. Case closed... until physicists learned to send one photon, then one electron, at a time.

Each particle hits the screen as a single dot. But let thousands accumulate and the interference stripes reappear — built up one lonely dot at a time. A single indivisible particle behaves as if it passed through both slits and interfered with itself.

  • Wave–particle duality — quanta are neither classical waves nor classical balls; they are something that does both
  • Superposition — until measured, the particle takes every path at once, described by a probability amplitude ψ
  • The measurement problem — put a detector at the slits to learn the path, and the superposition collapses: the fringes disappear
  • It works for matter — electrons, neutrons, even large molecules show the same interference

Richard Feynman called this "a phenomenon which is impossible … to explain in any classical way, and which has in it the heart of quantum mechanics. In reality, it contains the only mystery."

Young, T. (1804). The Bakerian Lecture. Phil. Trans. R. Soc. · Feynman, R. P. (1965). The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Vol. III, Ch. 1.

The Number That Ends an Epidemic

Episode 07
Epidemiology Herd Immunity Differential Equations

An epidemic never infects everyone — it stalls while most people are still healthy. Watch a crowd catch a disease, find the exact moment the outbreak turns, and vaccinate just enough people to stop it from ever starting.

The SIR model — Kermack & McKendrick, 1927

Split a population into three groups: those who can still catch the disease (Susceptible), those who have it (Infected), and those who can no longer catch or spread it (Recovered). Two rates move people between the groups — β, how fast the infection jumps from person to person, and γ, how fast the sick recover. That is the entire model.

From those two numbers comes the most famous quantity in epidemiology: R₀ = β/γ, the number of people one case infects in a population where nobody is immune. Above 1, the epidemic grows. Below 1, it dies.

  • The turning point — an outbreak peaks not when people get careful, but when so many have recovered that the effective reproduction number Rₑ = R₀·S/N falls through 1. The curve bends on its own
  • Herd immunity — immunise a fraction 1 − 1/R₀ of the population and Rₑ starts below 1 on day one. The epidemic never takes off, and the unvaccinated are protected too
  • Why measles is different — with R₀ ≈ 15, over 93% must be immune. With flu at R₀ ≈ 1.3, roughly a quarter suffices. The threshold is set by the pathogen, not by policy
  • The crowd is not the equation — here the curves emerge from individuals meeting individuals, so they run slower and infect fewer than the classical equations predict: real contacts are never perfectly mixed

Kermack and McKendrick wrote these equations to explain why the plague of Bombay faded away long before it ran out of people. Their answer — that an epidemic dies of its own success, having burned through enough susceptibles — is why vaccination works at all.

Kermack, W. O. & McKendrick, A. G. (1927). A Contribution to the Mathematical Theory of Epidemics. Proc. R. Soc. Lond. A, 115(772), 700–721.

Draw Anything. Circles Will Draw It Back.

Episode 08
Fourier Series Harmonic Analysis Signal Processing

Stack enough spinning circles, each turning at a whole-number rate, and the last one traces any closed curve you like. Draw your own shape and watch a chain of epicycles reproduce it — then take circles away and see exactly what breaks.

Fourier's series — 1822, and the backbone of every signal you have ever heard

Joseph Fourier was trying to describe how heat spreads through a metal bar. He claimed that any function, however jagged, could be written as a sum of pure sines and cosines. His contemporaries thought it was nonsense. Lagrange objected. It took a century to make rigorous — and it turned out to underpin audio, images, radio, MRI and quantum mechanics.

Read a closed curve as a complex function f(t), and its coefficients are cn = ∫ f(t)·e−2πint dt. Each cn is a vector of length |cn| turning n times per cycle. Chain them tip to tail, and the final tip draws the curve.

  • n is a speed, not a place — harmonic n is simply the circle that completes n turns while the pen completes one lap
  • Smoothness sets the price — a corner forces the coefficients to decay slowly (≈1/n² for a square); an analytic curve collapses much faster (≈1/n³ here), so a handful of circles already suffice
  • Symmetry punches holes in the spectrum — a square has energy only at n ≡ 1 (mod 4): 1, 5, 9, −3, −7… so raising N from 1 to 2 changes literally nothing. A five-pointed star keeps only n ≡ 1 (mod 5)
  • The error is exact, not estimated — Parseval's theorem says the energy you discard is precisely Σ|n|>N|cn|², which is what the readout reports

This is the same decomposition that lets an MP3 throw away the harmonics you cannot hear, and that lets a radio pick one station out of the noise. Here you can throw them away yourself, one circle at a time, and watch what it costs.

Fourier, J.-B. J. (1822). Théorie analytique de la chaleur. Firmin Didot, Paris. · Parseval, M.-A. (1806). Mémoire sur les séries. · Körner, T. W. (1988). Fourier Analysis. Cambridge University Press.

Why Wires Sing in the Wind

Episode 09
Fluid Dynamics Lattice Boltzmann Vortex Shedding

Nobody solved the Navier–Stokes equations here. Each cell of a 300×120 lattice holds nine numbers, relaxes them toward equilibrium, and passes them to its neighbours. Fluid dynamics is what happens next. Turn one knob and watch a still wake come alive.

The lattice-Boltzmann method — fluid mechanics as bookkeeping

Give every cell nine counters, one for each direction a fictitious particle might travel: still, north, south, east, west, and the four diagonals. Each step, relax the counters toward the equilibrium distribution set by the local density and velocity, then slide each counter one cell along its own direction. Collide, stream, repeat.

A Chapman–Enskog expansion proves that this bookkeeping reproduces the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations, with a kinematic viscosity ν = (1/ω − 1/2)/3 fixed by the relaxation rate alone. Nothing in the code knows what a vortex is.

  • Re is the only knob — Re = U·D/ν weighs inertia against viscosity. Everything else is bookkeeping
  • A sharp threshold — below Re ≈ 50 the wake stays attached: two vortices, stuck to the cylinder. Above it, the wake starts shedding them alternately, left and right. Measured here: at Re = 48 the oscillation dies away; at Re = 50 it settles onto a limit cycle
  • A Hopf bifurcation — just past the threshold the oscillation amplitude grows like √(Re − Re_c). Measured: A² is linear in Re, and extrapolating A² → 0 puts the threshold at 50.1
  • Strouhal, unbidden — the shedding frequency, made dimensionless as St = f·D/U, comes out at 0.20 near Re = 120. Experiment says ≈0.2 for a cylinder across five decades of Reynolds number. This code was never told

The textbook threshold for a cylinder in an unbounded stream is Re ≈ 47. Ours sits at 50 because the channel walls are close — a 16% blockage — exactly as they do in a real wind tunnel.

And the wire? Wind at 10 m/s past a 2 mm cable sheds vortices at f = St·U/D ≈ 1000 Hz. That is the note you hear. Vincenc Strouhal worked it out in 1878 by spinning wires on a frame and listening to them.

Frisch, U., Hasslacher, B. & Pomeau, Y. (1986). Lattice-gas automata for the Navier–Stokes equation. Phys. Rev. Lett. 56, 1505. · Qian, Y. H., d'Humières, D. & Lallemand, P. (1992). Lattice BGK models for Navier–Stokes equation. Europhys. Lett. 17, 479. · Williamson, C. H. K. (1996). Vortex dynamics in the cylinder wake. Annu. Rev. Fluid Mech. 28, 477. · Strouhal, V. (1878). Über eine besondere Art der Tonerregung. Ann. Phys. 241, 216.

Nobody Wants a Ghetto. Everybody Builds One.

Episode 10
Social Science Agent-Based Model Emergence

Give every household the mildest of preferences — I would like three of my ten neighbours to be like me — and let anyone who falls short move house. Nobody is a bigot. Nobody coordinates. Ask for 30% and the neighbourhood hands back 75%.

Schelling's segregation model — the gap between what we want and what we get

Two groups of equal size are scattered at random across a grid, with a tenth of the homes left empty. Every agent looks at its eight neighbours and asks a single question: is the share of neighbours like me at least τ? If yes, it stays. If no, it moves to a vacant home chosen at random. That is the entire rule. Nobody chooses a destination because of who lives there — the unhappy simply leave.

Thomas Schelling wrote it in 1971 with coins on a chessboard, and it earned him a share of the 2005 Nobel Prize in economics. The point is not that people are prejudiced. The point is that a preference far too weak to be called prejudice still produces a segregated city.

  • Ask for 30%, get 75% — measured here: a random mix scores S = 50.1%. Set τ = 0.30 and the grid settles, after 11 rounds, at S = 75.5%. Only 17.4% of agents were unhappy to begin with; that minority is enough to sort everybody
  • Micromotives are not macrobehaviour — no agent ever wanted a neighbourhood 75% like itself. Every one of them would have been content with 30%. The 45-point gap is manufactured entirely by the rule, by nobody's intention
  • A staircase, not a slope — an agent counts its like neighbours against however many neighbours it actually has, which is at most eight and fewer at an edge or beside an empty home. The like-fraction is a ratio of small whole numbers, so only certain values exist. τ = 0.34 and τ = 0.37 are therefore the very same demand, and settle identically: S = 78.8%, in exactly 10 rounds. Nudge τ to 0.38, just past 3/8, and the outcome jumps to S = 83.9%
  • Six of eight is the last demand that works — τ = 0.75 still settles, at S = 99.5%, after 61 rounds. τ = 0.76 asks for seven of eight: run it 2000 rounds and 91.5% of the neighbourhood is still relocating every single round, segregation stalled at 57.1%. Worse sorting than τ = 0.30 delivered, with 81.5% of agents unhappy on top. Demanding more returns less
  • Vacancy rescues the intolerant — that same τ = 0.80 grid does settle, at S = 99.9% and round 77, once 20% of the homes stand empty. Room to move is what makes an extreme demand satisfiable. Too little room jams everything instead: τ = 0.60 with 2% vacancy is still shuffling after 2000 rounds, though only 1.5% of agents relocate per round

The simulation will not tell you whether a given neighbourhood settles eventually. It cannot: at τ = 0.71 with 5% vacancy the moves per round plateau, tick upward, and only then collapse — the grid falls still at round 429. So the page reports what it sees, never what it foresees. "Settled" means nobody moved. "Still moving" means they were still moving when we last looked.

The uncomfortable corollary runs the other way too. If a segregated city is the aggregate of harmless individual preferences, then no individual can be blamed for it — and no individual, acting alone, can undo it.

Schelling, T. C. (1971). Dynamic models of segregation. Journal of Mathematical Sociology 1(2), 143–186. · Schelling, T. C. (1978). Micromotives and Macrobehavior. Norton. · Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel (2005), awarded jointly to Robert Aumann and Thomas Schelling.
